# Webinar and podcast planning formats

Both formats take the user's length, audience and goal. Segment durations are proposed by the skill to fit the user's total and are labelled as a draft to confirm. No number in these formats is a benchmark.

## A. Webinar plan

### Inputs (ask only what is missing)
- Topic, audience by role and company type, the goal (teach, show a product, open sales conversations), total length, platform, speakers by role.
- The data the opening will use. Ask: "Do you have your own figure or a named source for the opening? If not, I will open on a real situation instead." Never supply a statistic.
- Speaker credentials: only what each speaker confirms, otherwise "[User to add: speaker credentials the speaker confirms]".

### Structure
1. Opening: a hook built on the user's data with its source, or on a real situation the audience recognises; the agenda; who is speaking and why they are qualified (confirmed facts only).
2. Main content in three sections: the core idea, the method, an implementation or case (the case comes from the user; a customer case needs the customer's approval).
3. Close: the takeaways, the move into questions, one clear next step that is not a hard pitch.

For each section: two or three slide titles, the talking points, one interactive element (a poll, a chat question, a short demo following the stage demo rules), and the transition line into the next section.

Engagement prompts: plan several through the session, each tied to a section; the user decides how many.

### Output
A run of show table (time, duration, segment, speaker, slide titles, talking points, interaction, transition), segment times adding up to the total, then the open items list.

## B. Podcast episode plan

### Inputs
- Episode length, topic, guest by role and expertise (public professional facts only), listeners, the one message the episode should leave.
- What the guest has said in public before; what is off limits.

### Default arc (a starting shape; confirm it with the user)
1. A quick introduction of the guest and why this episode.
2. What it takes to win: strategies and first level insights from the guest's own work.
3. A deep dive into second order consequences: what happens after the obvious move, and how the guest measures it.
4. A sharp, concise close: the guest's one piece of advice and where listeners can find their public work.

### Questions
- Default for a one hour episode: five to seven main questions, plus follow-ups. Confirm the count with the user.
- Each question names what it is for and the follow-up if the answer is short.
- Backup questions: ask the user how many they want; write them tied to the arc.
- Transition lines between segments and time markers.

### Pre-show
Tech check, the guest's comfort and expectations, the reminder of off limits topics, recording consent.

### Guest briefing
Follow the plan, ask, draft order in speaker-notes-and-briefing.md.

### What this format never does
- No invented guest achievements, numbers or quotes.
- No "current landscape" filler: say what has changed for the listener and cite where that comes from.
