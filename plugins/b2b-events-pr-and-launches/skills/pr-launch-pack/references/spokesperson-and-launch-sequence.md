# Spokesperson protocol, launch day and launch sequence

Suggested defaults in this file are common choices offered to the user, who decides; they are never written into a plan as rules, and the plan says which defaults the user accepted. Until the user answers, each default appears in the plan as "[Suggested default, your decision: ...]" and is listed under open items.

## A. Spokesperson protocol
1. List every voice that will speak about the launch (executives, product leads, partners, company pages) by role.
2. Give each voice one distinct lane: the angle only they can speak to, the audience they reach, the vocabulary they use. Write it as a table:
| Voice (role) | Lane | Audience | Opening line (draft) | Approval status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
3. No two voices open with the same line or the same first idea. Check the openings side by side.
4. Every draft goes to its person for approval before publishing. Mark each "DRAFT FOR APPROVAL BY <role>". Never publish or schedule on anyone's behalf.
5. One core thesis runs through every piece, reworded per voice, so anyone who sees any one piece gets the main point.
6. Articles are not copies of the posts or of the release: each article takes a different angle, carries its own example, and opens with the answer first.
7. Engagement windows for each voice (how soon after publishing each one engages) are the user's decision. Suggested default, shown as a common choice the user decides on, never as a rule: the lead voices (for example the faculty or founders of a cohort launch) engage within about 2 hours of publishing, the other voices within about 3 to 5 hours. Ask whether the user wants this default or their own windows, and write whichever they choose.

## B. Launch day
- Decide the day from the user's own audience: when their buyers read, travel, close the quarter, or attend events, from the user's analytics or their sales team's knowledge.
- Do not choose a day because other companies launch on it, and do not state as fact which day other companies launch; if the user holds that view, record it as their view.
- Check the chosen date and weekday by script, and check it against holidays and events in the audience's region (ask the user to confirm the holiday list for each region).
- Sequence the release relative to other launch moments the user names (an event, an AMA, a customer webinar); the user decides the order. Suggested default when the launch has an AMA, shown as a common choice the user decides on: the press release goes out after the AMA and before the launch itself, so the release can carry what the AMA surfaced. Say it is a suggestion and ask.

## C. Teasers
- Tag each teaser by the signal it is meant to earn: a save, a comment, a share, or trust.
- Reveal enough to inform and create curiosity, never enough to replace the launch, and never with false scarcity or a declared catastrophe.
- Add an audience filter line when the launch is not for everyone (who should not attend or buy, as the user says; otherwise "[User to add: ...]").
- On launch day, switch teaser copy to "available today" wording.
- The number of teasers is the user's choice. Suggested default, shown as a common choice the user decides on: four teasers, each chasing a different signal (save, comment, share, trust). Offer it, ask, and use the user's number if they give one.

## D. Launch sequence plan
Built backwards from the user's launch date. The phase lengths come from the user's capacity; the skill proposes them as a draft.
1. Before launch: internal alignment and training; content written and approved; customer preview or beta feedback (only with real customers who agreed); partner outreach only for partners the user names; sales enablement materials; press preparation (release, media list, embargo); technical readiness; final checks and a rehearsal; the contingency plan.
2. Launch week: internal announcement; customer and partner notice; public announcement; press follow-up; community posts (see community-forums-and-supporters.md).
3. After launch: performance tracking against the user's own measures; customer feedback; issue fixes; a review of what to repeat.
For each item: owner (role), dependency, due date (weekday checked by script), status.

Success measures: the user defines them per category (awareness, adoption, revenue, customer satisfaction, timeline kept). The skill never supplies a benchmark rate or target.

Contingency plan: what happens if a dependency slips (the release is not approved, a demo breaks, a partner pulls out), who decides, and the fallback date.

## E. Award entries and forms
Capture each field's limit and type first; use only facts the user supplies; label the customer stage truthfully; keep tables whole; count each field by script.
